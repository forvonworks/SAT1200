# Book 2 — Lesson 18: Study Design & Statistical Claims — Problem Sets

**Source content file**, paired with `concepts.md`. This lesson is reasoning-based, not
computational — every item is a short study scenario followed by a question like "what
can be concluded?" The answer key gives the correct reasoning in one or two sentences,
not a number. Every scenario+answer pair below was re-checked against the standard
methodology rules (random sampling → generalization; random assignment → causation;
neither → neither) before finalizing — there is no numeric safety net for this lesson,
so this reasoning check stands in for the script-verification used elsewhere.

---

## Set 1 — Random Sample or Convenience Sample?

For each scenario, state whether the sample is random or a convenience/self-selected
sample, and say what that means for generalizing the result.

1. A phone manufacturer emails a survey to 2,000 customers chosen by a computer at
   random from its complete list of 400,000 registered owners.
2. A radio show asks listeners to call in and share their opinion on a new tax law; 300
   people call in.
3. A hospital randomly selects 150 patient files from its full database of 10,000
   patients treated last year to study average recovery time.
4. A gym posts a poll on its own Instagram page asking followers which days they prefer
   to work out; 600 followers respond.
5. A national park ranger stops every 20th car entering the park (regardless of who is
   in it) to ask visitors how far they traveled, until 400 cars have been surveyed.

## Set 2 — Experiment or Observational Study?

For each scenario, state whether it is an experiment (random assignment) or an
observational study, and say whether the conclusion can claim causation or only
correlation/association.

1. Researchers randomly assign 80 plants to receive either a new fertilizer or plain
   water, then measure growth after 3 weeks.
2. A teacher notices that students who sit in the front row tend to have higher test
   scores than students who sit in the back, based on where students chose to sit.
3. A pharmaceutical trial randomly assigns 300 volunteers to receive either a new
   medication or a placebo, with neither the volunteers nor the doctors told which is
   which.
4. An economist studies 10 years of national data and finds that months with higher ice
   cream sales also have higher rates of sunburn-related doctor visits.
5. A company lets employees choose whether to work from home or come into the office,
   then compares the two (self-chosen) groups' productivity scores at year's end.

## Set 3 — Mixed: Sample AND Assignment Together

Using the four-row table from Concept 3, identify both (a) whether the sample was
random and (b) whether assignment was random, then state exactly what can be concluded.

1. A university randomly selects 500 students from its full enrollment to participate,
   then randomly assigns half to a new 15-minute daily study technique and half to
   their usual routine, measuring final exam scores.
2. A fitness blogger's 40 newsletter subscribers volunteer for a step-count challenge;
   the blogger randomly assigns half to use a pedometer app and half not to, comparing
   average daily steps after one month.
3. A city randomly selects 1,000 households from its utility billing records to survey
   about energy use; households are simply asked whether they already use solar panels
   or not, and energy bills are compared.
4. A psychology class of 25 students (not randomly chosen from any larger population)
   is observed: students who reported sleeping over 8 hours the night before a quiz
   scored higher than those who reported less, with no groups assigned by the
   researcher.

## Set 4 — Spot the Overreach

Each item gives a conclusion that goes further than the study's design can support.
Name exactly which rule is broken (sampling, assignment, or both) and restate a
properly-scoped conclusion.

1. A cooking website surveys 90 people who voluntarily signed up for its newsletter
   about a new recipe, finds 70 liked it, and claims: *"This recipe is a hit with home
   cooks everywhere."*
2. Researchers randomly assign 200 randomly-selected adults nationwide to a 10-minute
   daily meditation group or a no-meditation group, and find the meditation group
   reports lower stress. A wellness influencer summarizes it as: *"Studies show
   meditation makes some people feel calmer"* — intentionally vague, to be evaluated
   for whether it actually *understates* what the study supports.
3. An observational study randomly samples 2,000 adults nationally and finds that
   adults who read for pleasure weekly report better memory test scores than those who
   don't. A newspaper headline states: *"Reading causes better memory in adults."*
4. A single high school (not randomly chosen) runs an experiment: it randomly assigns
   its own 120 students to a new homework app or the old paper method, finding the app
   group's grades improve. The school district announces: *"This app will improve
   grades for every student in the state."*
5. A convenience sample of 50 shoppers at one mall is observed (no groups assigned):
   shoppers who used a store's loyalty app spent more per visit than those who didn't.
   A marketing email claims: *"Our loyalty app causes customers to spend more,
   everywhere."*

## Set 5 — Write the Correct Conclusion

Given each study design, write in your own words exactly what the study *can* support
and what it *cannot* — there is no single fixed answer format, but your reasoning must
match the sample/assignment rules from Concept 3.

1. A random sample of 600 adults nationwide is surveyed (no groups assigned) about
   coffee consumption and self-reported alertness; those who drink 2+ cups daily report
   higher alertness.
2. A biology lab randomly assigns 60 (non-randomly-recruited, volunteer) lab mice to a
   new vitamin supplement or a control diet, finding the supplement group has measurably
   better bone density after 8 weeks.
3. A random sample of 1,200 high school students nationwide is surveyed about whether
   they play a musical instrument and their standardized test scores; students who play
   an instrument score higher on average, with no groups assigned.

## Set 6 — Bonus / Challenge (layered scenarios)

1. A university randomly samples 800 students from its enrollment to survey about
   part-time work and GPA — but of the 800 contacted, only 110 (a self-selected
   subgroup) actually respond to the survey. The university concludes the result
   represents "the typical student." What's the real design here, and is the conclusion
   justified?
2. A nationally representative random sample of 3,000 adults is recruited for a sleep
   study. Within that sample, researchers randomly assign half to a new sleep-tracking
   device and half to a paper sleep diary, and find the device group reports more
   consistent sleep schedules. Is a nationwide causal claim justified here, and why does
   this case differ from most of the studies above?
3. A trick case: a snack company randomly selects 500 of its registered app users (not
   the general public, but a random sample *of its own user base*) and randomly assigns
   them to try a new flavor or the classic flavor, finding the new flavor rated higher.
   The company claims: "Among our app users, the new flavor is preferred, and we can't
   yet say whether that holds for people who've never used our app." Is this specific,
   narrower claim actually justified, unlike the other overreaching claims in this
   lesson?

## Answer Key

**Set 1:**
1. Random — every owner on the full list had an equal chance of selection, so the
   result can be generalized to all 400,000 owners (within sampling error).
2. Convenience/self-selected — only listeners motivated enough to call in are
   represented; cannot be generalized to all listeners or the public.
3. Random — files were randomly selected from the complete patient database, so results
   generalize to all patients treated that year.
4. Convenience/self-selected — only the gym's own Instagram followers who chose to
   respond are represented; cannot be generalized to all gym members or the public.
5. Random — stopping every 20th car regardless of who's inside gives every visiting car
   (and thus every visitor group) an equal, systematic chance of selection; this
   generalizes to park visitors during the survey period.

**Set 2:**
1. Experiment (random assignment) — causation can be claimed: the fertilizer caused the
   growth difference (within this study's plants).
2. Observational study — only correlation/association can be claimed; front-row seating
   wasn't randomly assigned, so other factors (e.g., more engaged students choosing the
   front row) could explain the score difference.
3. Experiment (random assignment) — causation can be claimed: the medication's effect
   (within the trial's volunteer population) is supported.
4. Observational study — only correlation/association; a lurking variable (hot weather)
   drives both, ice cream sales don't cause sunburn visits.
5. Observational study — only correlation/association; employees self-selected their
   work location, so other differences between self-motivated remote workers and
   office-choosers could explain the productivity gap, not the location itself.

**Set 3:**
1. Random sample + random assignment → the strongest case: causation can be claimed
   AND generalized to the full university population.
2. Not a random sample (self-selected newsletter volunteers) + random assignment →
   causation can be claimed only for these 40 subscribers, not generalized to all
   gym-goers or the public.
3. Random sample + NOT random assignment (households chose solar panels themselves) →
   only correlation can be claimed, but it can be generalized to the city's households
   as an association (solar-panel users tend to have different energy bills) — not a
   causal claim.
4. Not a random sample (one specific class) + NOT random assignment (no groups
   assigned, just observed) → weakest case: only an association within this one class
   can be noted; it cannot be generalized to other students, and no causal claim about
   sleep and quiz scores is justified.

**Set 4:**
1. Sampling violated — self-selected newsletter volunteers, not a random sample of
   "home cooks everywhere." Correct: "Most of the 90 volunteers who tried the recipe
   liked it."
2. Neither rule is broken here — if anything, "some people feel calmer" slightly
   understates what a random-sample, random-assignment study actually supports (a
   generalizable causal claim for the population sampled); flagged to show that not
   every summary is an overreach — some are simply cautious restatements of a
   well-supported result.
3. Assignment violated — this is an observational study (no groups assigned), so only
   correlation/association can be claimed, even though the sample was random. Correct:
   "Reading for pleasure is associated with better memory scores in adults."
4. Sampling violated — the causal claim is fine *for this school's own 120 students*
   (random assignment was used), but the school itself wasn't randomly selected from
   "every student in the state," so the result cannot be generalized state-wide.
5. Both rules violated — convenience sample (one mall) means no generalizing "everywhere,"
   and no random assignment (shoppers self-selected into app use) means only
   correlation, not causation, even within that mall's shoppers.

**Set 5:**
1. Can support: an association between coffee consumption and self-reported alertness,
   generalizable to adults nationwide (random sample). Cannot support: that coffee
   *causes* higher alertness (no random assignment — more alert people might simply
   choose to drink more coffee, or another lurking variable could explain both).
2. Can support: a causal claim that the supplement improves bone density, but only for
   mice like the ones in this study's volunteer pool (random assignment, but not a
   random sample from "all mice" in any formal population sense). Cannot support: that
   every mouse (or any other species) would show the same effect.
3. Can support: an association between playing an instrument and higher test scores,
   generalizable to high school students nationwide (random sample). Cannot support:
   that playing an instrument *causes* the higher scores (no random assignment — a
   lurking variable such as access to resources, family support, or prior academic
   interest could explain both).

**Set 6:**
1. The real design is weaker than a true random sample: it started as a random sample
   of 800, but only a self-selected 110 actually responded (a classic "non-response"
   problem — the final respondents are no longer guaranteed representative, since
   people with strong opinions or more free time may be more likely to respond). The
   conclusion that the result represents "the typical student" is not fully justified;
   the true usable sample is the self-selected 110 respondents, not the originally
   intended random 800.
2. Yes — a nationwide causal claim is justified here, because both conditions are
   met: the 3,000 adults were a nationally representative random sample AND the device
   vs. diary assignment was random. This differs from most studies in this lesson,
   which typically satisfy only one of the two conditions (a random sample without
   random assignment, or random assignment within a non-random volunteer group) — this
   is the rare case occupying the top-left cell of the Concept 3 table.
3. Yes — this narrower claim is fully justified and is a good model of properly-scoped
   language: the sample was random, but only *from the company's own user base*, not
   from the general public, so the company correctly limits its causal claim ("among
   our app users") to the population it actually sampled from, and explicitly declines
   to generalize beyond it. This is the opposite of every flawed conclusion in Set 4 —
   it shows that a study can be used correctly by simply matching the claim's scope to
   the sample's actual scope.
